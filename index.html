Price the six-week reserve before the app does

A delivery app can end a driver's week with one email. There is no manager to argue with, just a deactivation notice and a 72 hour appeal window, and every hour after that window closes makes reinstatement less likely. The platforms never state what a deactivation costs, so the driver has to state it first: around six weeks of pay gone while the appeal runs, sometimes for good. That number is not a footnote to the hourly rate. It is a line item, and it can be funded in advance.

Take a driver grossing $1,204 a month across two delivery apps, $724 on the first and $480 on the second, on 68 active hours. The costs come off before any rate gets quoted: fuel $148, the commercial insurance rider $96, wear at $0.09 a mile across 1,300 miles = $117, and $37 for the data line the apps run on. 148 + 96 + 117 + 37 = $398. Net is 1,204 - 398 = $806 a month. On 68 hours that is 806 / 68 = $11.85 an hour, against the $17.71 gross figure in the app's own weekly summary.

A deactivation stops the $806 cold. Six weeks of net works out to 806 x 12 / 52 = $186 a week, so the outage price is 6 x 186 = $1,116. The driver who quotes $11.85 an hour and banks none of it is not really earning $11.85; he is lending his safety margin to the platform interest free. Funded at $93 a week, exactly half the weekly net, the reserve is complete in 1,116 / 93 = 12 weeks and then stops costing anything.

Then the email arrives. An appeal filed inside the 72 hours costs nothing but the wait, and the reserve does the work. If the account comes back at week three, 3 x 186 = $558 of the fund survives as the start of the next buffer. If it never comes back, the $1,116 is self-paid severance that funds the move to a third app instead of a panic. Six weeks of pay is the real price of carrying the account. Pay it ahead of the email, and the worst day the platform can schedule still nets $11.85 an hour.
